🧠 Key Concepts

How Work Is Managed in a Company

After joining, a developer becomes part of a team of perhaps 10, 20 or 30 people. The team has to decide what is done first, what comes later, and how the work is tracked. Work doesn't arrive as an informal instruction from one person — companies use a defined process and tools. The class covered the project-management methodology and a developer's day-to-day activities; there are two broad approaches: Waterfall and Agile.

  • The customer/client provides the requirements; the architect/design team analyses them and prepares the design; developers implement it; the build goes to the testing team; after testing it moves to a higher environment and finally to production. Later changes and fixes are maintenance.
  • The developer's role here is mainly implementation — the architect says what to build. Code review and code coverage belong to implementation.
  • The testing loop: software can still have bugs. If testing finds a blocker, the build goes back to development, the developer supplies a new build, and testing runs again — until the build is stable.
Why Waterfall Becomes Difficult — and How Agile Differs

Waterfall expects all requirements up front. In the instructor's example the customer spends months on a requirements document, the architect designs, developers spend even longer implementing — and meanwhile the customer's needs change, or the architect misunderstood part of a product that was hard to picture in advance. His analogy: the customer wants one thing, and after all those phases the product delivered turns out to be something different.

Agile is a flexible, iterative way of working: instead of every requirement at the start, the team picks a smaller set it can finish in a short period. The team may work together for a long time but plans and delivers a manageable slice each cycle.

AspectWaterfallAgile
RequirementsFinalised early, expected in fullA small set chosen for each short cycle
ChangeInflexible; expensive once development is far alongWelcomed — flexible, iterative
Customer feedbackDelayed — result seen after a long timeEarly — after each working increment; the customer can suggest changes or new features
ReleasesOne large releaseSmaller, more frequent releases
RiskA misunderstanding can affect the whole projectFound early, so there is less to redo
SuitsStable, well-defined requirementsFast-changing or innovative projects

An Agile cycle: understand the requirement → design → develop → verify → demo to the customer. It lasts one, two or three weeks; many companies use two-week sprints, which means 10 working days, not 14 calendar days.

🔥 Interview angle: "Waterfall vs Agile?" — answer with the table: requirements up front vs per cycle, late vs early feedback, one big release vs frequent ones.
Scrum — the Framework Used Under Agile

Scrum is a framework under Agile; Kanban was mentioned as another approach, and the class focuses on Scrum. A Scrum team plans only the work it believes it can finish within a sprint (one to four weeks; the example was two weeks / 10 working days).

RoleResponsibility
Product Owner (PO)Main point of contact for product requirements: collects them from customers, users, stakeholders and the business, and communicates priorities to the technical team.
Scrum MasterFacilitates the Scrum process and the daily stand-up. May or may not be technical — the role is to facilitate the process and meetings, not necessarily to build the software.
Development TeamCarries out the work — developers, architects and QA/testing participants.
The Daily Stand-up (DSM)
  • Held every working day, short — about 15–20 minutes (up to 30 at the extreme). Standing keeps it short instead of becoming a long seated meeting.
  • Each member says: (1) what I did yesterday, (2) what I'll do today, (3) any blockers.
  • Example: yesterday you finished an API; today you'll integrate it with the database; if the database server isn't available, that is a blocker and you raise it in the meeting.
  • The instructor stressed communicating blockers: if a dependency is missing or another team hasn't responded, say it clearly. Don't wait silently or assume someone else knows.
Communication and Professional Behaviour
  • Ask when you don't know — raise your hand; don't keep blockers to yourself. Ask through the company/team, not through friends outside it.
  • Answer emails promptly. If you aren't the right contact, reply and point the sender to the right person; don't leave mails unanswered for days.
  • Be transparent: if a colleague promised to share database details, mention that update in the team meeting. Managers and teammates need visibility into progress and blockers.
  • Prepare a few notes before the stand-up so your update is clear.
  • Don't say "yes" to every task at once. If you need to investigate, ask for time to research and then give a realistic commitment — don't promise a delivery time before you understand the task.
The Scrum Events
EventWhenWhat happens
Sprint PlanningStart of the sprintThe team reviews the requirements and decides what it can realistically complete; the PO helps prioritise. Capacity, holidays, leave and other commitments are considered.
Daily Stand-upEvery dayYesterday, today, blockers.
Sprint ReviewEnd of the sprintThe team demonstrates the completed work to the PO/customer. Not every selected item may be finished; incomplete work moves to a later sprint — "spillover".
Sprint RetrospectiveAfter the sprintWhat went well? What didn't? How can we improve? Every member takes part — e.g. good teamwork, a delayed database dependency, work that spilled over — and the team picks improvements for the next sprint.
Product Backlog, User Stories and Sprint Backlog
  • The Product Backlog is the list of product requirements, collected and organised by the Product Owner.
  • Requirements are often written as user stories: "As a user, I want to log in with my user ID and password so that the system lets me in"; "As a user, I should be able to search for products on the platform"; "As a customer, I want to add a product to my cart so that I can purchase it."
  • The team doesn't take everything into one sprint — it selects what it can finish. That selection is the Sprint Backlog; the team then breaks the stories into technical tasks and plans the implementation.

A story may have several tasks (roughly 5–10 as an example). For a product-search story: enable caching, query the database, format the results, apply filters.

Story Points
  • Agile teams don't simply say "this takes N days". Story points estimate relative complexity or effort.
  • Example values: 1 = very easy, 3 = medium, 5 = complex, 8 = very complex — based on the Fibonacci sequence (13 also mentioned).
  • The team compares its capacity with the points it believes it can complete, keeping some buffer for helping others or unexpected work.
  • Closing example: a user can calculate a discount, and if a coupon is already applied the system reports the coupon as invalid / only one coupon per user. A small task might get 1 point; a more complex one a larger estimate.
🔥 Interview angle: a story point is not officially a number of days. People loosely say "one point ≈ one day" when explaining, but the real idea is relative complexity — the instructor repeated this twice.

Jira and Confluence
  • Jira is a project-management and issue-tracking tool used to track the work assigned to each person; managers check Jira for status instead of asking everyone informally.
  • Basic workflow: To Do (assigned, not started) → In Progress → Done.
  • Tickets carry comments and updates. Record blockers and dependencies in the ticket — e.g. "waiting for another team to provide a database" — so there is a history of why work was delayed and what is pending.
  • Confluence is for documentation and collaboration — project documents live there rather than only in local Word files. Some companies use SharePoint or other tools instead.
  • Jira, Confluence, GitHub/Bitbucket and Jenkins can be integrated as part of the development workflow.
Q&A — Certifications

A student asked about certifications. Some companies or clients look for certifications such as AWS or Azure; they can help demonstrate or compare knowledge, but the instructor stressed they are not enough on their own — practical expertise matters.

💡 Added clarification:
  • Velocity and planning poker: teams usually track how many story points they complete per sprint (velocity) and use it to decide how much to take next time; estimating by discussion with cards is called planning poker.
  • The official Scrum Guide sets the Daily Scrum at 15 minutes, and the three questions are a popular format rather than a rule; the class's 15–20 (up to 30) minutes is the practical range.
  • Kanban isn't built on sprints: work flows continuously across a board (To Do → In Progress → Done), usually with a limit on work in progress.

✅ Action Items

Suggested practice (added — no homework was assigned in this recording):
  • Write three user stories for your e-commerce project in the "As a … I want … so that …" form, and break one into tasks.
  • Work out the capacity of a team of 6 over a two-week sprint with one person on leave for 3 days.
  • Write a 30-second stand-up update (yesterday / today / blockers) for the work you did this week.
  • Explain "Waterfall vs Agile" and "why a story point isn't a day" out loud in under two minutes.
