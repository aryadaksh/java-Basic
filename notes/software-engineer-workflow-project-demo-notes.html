🏢 Part 1 — The Software Engineer Workflow

Where Does the Work Come From?

The instructor opened with a question: you join a company (TCS was the example) as a developer — who gives you the work? His explanation: TCS is a service-based company (Tata Consultancy Services — a consultancy), so it doesn't originate the work itself. The work comes from another company. He described product-based companies (PBC) as ones that hire relatively few, highly paid people — his example: a need for 10,000 employees but hiring only ~500 — who take the decisions and oversee the work, then hand work to service companies. In the industry that company is called the client.

⚠️ Take This as a Simplified Picture

The service-vs-product split was explained informally (and the audio around it is garbled). Real companies are more mixed — product companies also hire many engineers, and service companies also build products. Use it as the instructor's mental model, not a precise definition.

The Product Owner

The person who gives you the requirement is the Product Owner — the owner of the requirement document (like the e-commerce project PDF). He described this person as a mix of technical and non-technical, very good at communication. They meet the client, understand the need, and draft the requirement. When developers finish work, they present it to the Product Owner, who checks with the client whether it's as expected. For an engineer, the client is the Product Owner.

In Q&A he clarified: both the vendor side and the client side can have product owners. "Product owner" doesn't mean owner of the company — it's whoever is responsible for that particular product or requirement, including for work on an existing product that your team enhances.

Requirements Come in Slices — Sprints

A requirement might be six months of work, but you never take it all at once. You ask the Product Owner: "Out of this, what's the highest-priority work you want to see in the next 15 days?" The whole team builds that slice, shows it to the Product Owner (and possibly the client). If it's fine, the next slice begins; if there's feedback, you fix it. In Agile, this cycle is called a sprint.

Sprint lengthInstructor's note
~1 week (5 days)Common in startups — requirement Monday, result Friday
2 weeksMost common for intermediate-pace companies
3 weeks"Little chill" environments — e.g. Monday start, ending the Friday of the third week

In industry, Monday–Friday counts as one week, not seven days — a "2-week sprint" means 10 working days. He had worked in companies with both one-week and two-week sprints.

  1. Requirement is the input. A developer doesn't just start coding — he'll have hundreds of questions, discussed with the Product Owner and team before development starts. Once the doubts are cleared, development begins.
  2. Unit test cases. After development you verify your own work before handing it over (JUnit — "we'll understand later"). Your immediate customer is QA (the testing team).
  3. Check in to a repository (Git or Bitbucket) — you can't rely on your own machine, so code is checked in continuously.
  4. Build. Compile the source code and create a JAR so it can run on the QA machine — QA won't have Eclipse, and often uses Linux boxes. They run the JAR with a command, giving the JAR name and the input. Maven (coming later) will explain "build" properly.
  5. QA works in parallel. Their input is also the requirement: while you develop, they write test cases — e.g. for a banking app: payee name must not contain alphanumeric characters; minimum/maximum limits must be enforced; an added payee should appear in the drop-down; selecting it leads to the amount screen and the fund transfer. They then execute these one by one on your build.
  6. Bugs. "We are not perfect" — QA reports issues (the example: "I observed 20 issues") through Jira.
  7. Fix → rebuild → retest — multiple iterations between developer and QA until QA certifies the build.
  8. Environments: certified work moves on to SIT, then pre-prod, then prod.
Added detail (standard, not stated in class): the JAR is typically run as java -jar <name>.jar. The instructor only said "JAR command, give the JAR name, give the input" and deferred details to Maven.
Jira — Why Bugs Are Recorded, Not Spoken

Testing and development teams are often in different cities — and even if they sit next to each other, nobody trusts spoken words. His example: a tester tells Ayush "AirPay is not working"; Ayush is on leave for three days; when the team later asks about it, "I told Ayush" doesn't count. So the bug is properly recorded: a subject line, details, which data fails, a screenshot — and assigned to a person. If that person is absent, it can be reassigned to someone else. Now it's a tracked responsibility, and each morning the team can see how many issues are open.

  • Jira is a task/bug/project tracking web tool. Tickets have an ID and sit on a board (To Do → In Progress → In Review → Done), with the assignee's photo, the reporter's name, filters, and more.
  • When an issue is assigned to you, you get an email notification, and you're now the owner.
  • You must write a comment even if the bug is wrong: if valid — "I've started work, will keep you posted"; if you need more information — say so and offer to connect on a call; if invalid — explain that the functionality is implemented as designed and mark it "Invalid and closed."
  • Requirements themselves also get converted into Jira tickets (stories/tasks).
"I'll Just Pick Work From Jira" Is Not the Day-to-Day

Many freshers describe their day as "go to the office and pick a task from Jira." The instructor corrected this: you were present when the requirement was converted into tickets, so you already understand the whole scope. The next 10–15 days of tasks are "running in your brain," and you pick the high-priority ones to start. It's not like checking a flight status and boarding.

What Joining a Company Is Really Like
  • You're not asked to write "an Amazon system" on day one — work is divided into small requirements.
  • There will be people ready to support you. His reasoning: a teammate doing five tasks a day and overloaded is exactly why you were hired; if they help you for one or two months, you'll take on tasks (e.g. they hand you two of their three).
  • You'll be given a lot of time — get friendly and ask questions. "Build a habit of asking questions, be it right or wrong."
  • Learn the process. The actual work can be tiny — sometimes one line — but that one line can be critical: "out of 20,000 Java files, where to change that one line?" That takes real understanding.
  • He used a deliberately exaggerated line — that in a year you might write only ~100 lines of code — to stress that understanding the process matters as much as typing code. Treat the number as emphasis, not a statistic.

🤖 Part 2 — AI Usage in the Industry (Instructor's Account)

The Interview Question: "How Much of Your Code Is Written With AI?"

During the code reviews the instructor asked students whether their code was AI-written, then said an interviewer will ask exactly this. His account: companies (he said in India, and "my company") ask engineers to maintain roughly 30% of code written using AI; usage is tracked through Git commits (you indicate which code was AI-generated) and reported weekly. His suggested interview answer: "My company asks us to maintain about 30% — we follow that."

  • He cautioned against treating casual claims like "100% AI" or "everyone will get 50 lakh" as company policy: "People talk casually over chai. I'm talking about the policy." Nobody says 100% as official policy.
  • He also mentioned that some people, to meet such quotas, report code they wrote themselves as AI-written.
⚠️ Verify Before Repeating This
  • The 30% figure and Git-based tracking are the instructor's account from his own experience — policies differ widely between companies, and some forbid or restrict AI tools entirely. Don't quote "30%" as an industry standard.
  • The lines about layoffs ("they plan to remove 30–40% of people") were garbled in the audio and aren't reliably captured here.
  • Reporting your real AI usage accurately is the safer professional habit than adjusting numbers to hit a target.
Why This Matters for Your Own Practice

In the reviews he repeatedly asked students to explain AI-assisted code line by line — and several couldn't. Being able to explain your own project (what a repository does, where an exception is thrown, why a loop is used) is what the interview will test, whatever tools you used to write it.

🛒 Part 3 — The E-Commerce Mini Project Demos

The Format

Ten students volunteered; the target was about 3 minutes each, running until the 11 o'clock cutoff. Each student demoed the running app, then showed the package structure, then concluded their learning. The instructor interrupted with questions during the code walkthroughs, interview-style.

What the Demos Had in Common
  • Packages: model, repository, service, exception, main (some added util or a test package).
  • Admin and Customer menus; a "guest/explore" mode where you can view and search products and add to a cart, but must register or log in to place an order.
  • Product search by name (partial text like "AXY" finding "Galaxy"), ID, category, brand, and price range; place order, cancel order, view my orders, update my address.
  • Pre-loaded sample data so nothing has to be re-entered each run.
🔥 Instructor's Observations

He noticed everyone ended up with exactly 5 exception classes ("not 3, not 6") and that ~99% of the code was very similar across students. Since the requirement PDF specified the five exceptions and the layout, similarity is partly expected — but it's the cue that interviewers will probe your understanding rather than your output.

Code-Review Habit: Right-Click → References

When he wanted to know where an exception is used, he had the student right-click the class → References → Workspace in Eclipse; it lists every place it's called. His advice: "When code review happens, never get stuck." Writing ~20 classes a day, you won't remember after 10 days where a method or exception is used — use this to navigate your own code quickly.

Vikas's Design Walkthrough — Four Good Practices
  1. Validate everything, then mutate. In place-order he first validated all the cart items; only after validation passed did he reduce inventory, and then finalized the order status. (This is the "validate-all-first" rule — never half-update stock.)
  2. Test the service layer before wiring the menu. He kept a separate test package: whenever he wrote part of OrderService, he tested it first, and only then connected it to the console menu in the main application.
  3. Input validation utility. Rather than repeating checks, he wrote a static InputUtil class (validate product ID — must be greater than zero, no negatives; validate price; stock quantity; customer/cart ID) and called it from the main menu code, instead of repeating Scanner checks everywhere.
  4. Constructor injection. Services receive their repositories from outside instead of creating them inside. Creating a dependency inside the class (e.g. new inside OrderService) is tightly coupled; supplying it from outside is loosely coupled. He mentioned constructor injection is what most people use in industry; setter injection also exists. The instructor added: this concept is what the Spring Framework is built around.

His learning summary: separate the layers — repository for data management (a Map keyed by ID with model objects as values), services for business logic — and use model objects to carry attributes like name, ID, email.

Illustrative code (reconstructed from the explanation, not shown in the transcript): 
// Tightly coupled — the class creates its own dependencies
class OrderService {
    private ProductRepository productRepository = new ProductRepository();
    private CustomerRepository customerRepository = new CustomerRepository();
}

// Loosely coupled — constructor injection: dependencies supplied from outside
class OrderService {
    private final ProductRepository productRepository;
    private final CustomerRepository customerRepository;

    OrderService(ProductRepository productRepository, CustomerRepository customerRepository) {
        this.productRepository = productRepository;
        this.customerRepository = customerRepository;
    }
}

// In main — you decide which repositories to create and pass them in
ProductRepository productRepo = new ProductRepository();
CustomerRepository customerRepo = new CustomerRepository();
OrderService orderService = new OrderService(productRepo, customerRepo);
// Static block seeding sample data so you don't re-enter products every run
class ProductRepository {
    private static final Map<Integer, Product> products = new HashMap<>();

    static {
        products.put(101, new Product(101, "iPhone 16", "Mobiles", 79999, 10, "Apple"));
        products.put(102, new Product(102, "Galaxy S24", "Mobiles", 69999, 15, "Samsung"));
    }
}
What the Students Said They Learned
  • How different classes connect and communicate — and, for non-technical growth, handling time pressure.
  • Five-package separation: main (user interface calling services), service (business logic), repository (storage area), model (data), exception.
  • The hardest part: passing objects between classes — one student had only built projects of 5–10 classes before; this one had packages with 20–30 classes.
  • Practicing concepts individually is different from making them all work in a single project.
  • Several asked for help from AI or peers on the cart flow; the instructor's repeated feedback was to improve communication while presenting.

🗺️ The Instructor's Roadmap — Baby Steps

Each Project Extends the Last

He framed this project as a "baby step" — the next project will be an extension of this one, and whether you do it is up to you, but if you skip it you won't understand the next one, and the final project even less. Coming up: JDBC → another project; Maven → another project; Spring Framework → another project — "every day you'll have to do that"; by the end, more than 25 projects like this. You can't escape practice "till complete Java."

The Thought Process This Project Builds
  • A real project has 50+ classes and 10–20 exception classes.
  • Classes interact with each other — designing those interactions is the job.
  • Thinking about static blocks for startup data.
  • The role of collections — how intermediate data is stored. Today you put data into collections and read it back; later, data comes from the database into collections, and you process it from there.

📌 Important Points

  • The requirement document is the input to everything — developers clarify doubts before coding.
  • Work arrives in sprints of prioritized slices, not as a six-month block.
  • Your immediate customer is QA; bugs are tracked in Jira and every assigned ticket needs a comment from you.
  • Code flows Local → Dev → QA → SIT/UAT → Pre-prod → Prod, and problems always loop back to Dev.
  • Understand the process, ask questions freely, and expect small-but-critical tasks at first.
  • Be able to explain every line of your project, whatever tools helped write it.
  • Validate everything before mutating state (place-order); test the service layer before the menu.
  • Prefer constructor injection over creating dependencies inside a class.
  • Use the concepts you've learned (static block, Comparator, exceptions) wherever they genuinely fit.
